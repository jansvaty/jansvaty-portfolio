Case Study - Product Design & Build

Kiwi Compass

Role

Product design, data model, front-end build, brand, user testing

Client

Personal project

Deliverables

Installable web app (PWA), 12-factor matching engine, brand identity

Year

2026

Overview

Which Australian city should a Kiwi actually move to?

Around 700,000 New Zealanders live in Australia, and thousands more make the move every year. Most pick their city on hearsay: a cousin in Brisbane, a forum thread, a friend's opinion of a place they visited once. It is a stressful, life-sized decision made with weekend-trip information.

I know the feeling first-hand. I have chosen a new city with a blank sheet more than once - Prague to British Columbia, Montreal, Calgary, Puerto Escondido, Auckland, Wellington - and I always wished a tool existed that would take my actual life and compare it against real data. So I designed and built one, end-to-end: research, data model, interface, brand, code, testing and deployment.

How it works

Nine questions, your own weighting, a transparent answer

Who you are, not who's average

Field of work, hobbies, weather preference, how you commute, where you holiday, social life, who's coming with you, the plan, and a rent-or-buy budget slider. Conditional factors - kids, pets, dating - only switch on when they apply.

You set the weights

Before the results, a slider screen asks how much each factor matters, from 0 to 10. The sliders are the scoring model: a factor at zero is removed from the maths entirely. No hidden weighting decides for you.

Reasoning, not a black box

Twelve Australian cities are ranked by percentage match, and every factor of every city explains its score in plain English - with a link to the official source behind it: ABS Census, ABS labour statistics, BOM climate data, BITRE aviation routes, Home Affairs.

The radar moment

Results open with a radar graph: your priorities as a filled shape, your best-matched city's fit as a dashed overlay. Wherever the dashed line dips inside your shape, the city under-delivers on something you care about. The chart explains the ranking at a glance.

Process

How the data and the "back end" actually work

There is no back end. The whole app is a handful of static files - HTML, CSS and JavaScript - served from GitHub Pages. Each of the twelve cities is a small profile of numbers and notes in a single data file: employment strength by industry, median rents and house prices, climate averages, direct flight routes, school, transport and pet-friendliness ratings. Every figure was compiled from an official source such as the ABS Census, the Bureau of Meteorology or BITRE, and the source is named next to it in the code and in the results.

When someone answers the questions, their own browser does the maths: each city's profile is scored against their answers, multiplied by their importance sliders, and totalled into a percentage. Nothing they enter ever leaves their device, because there is no server to send it to. A service worker caches the files, so the app keeps working offline and installs to a phone's home screen like a native app. I wrote it in plain JavaScript - no framework, no build step - with the help of Claude Pro as my build partner.

Testing

Two rounds of testing with five users

I tested the app with five users across two rounds. The sessions focused on four areas: how people set their priorities, how well the factors aligned with what someone relocating to Australia actually weighs up, the overall UI, and usability evaluated against usability heuristics.

Both rounds fed directly back into the product - refining the factor set, the weighting step and the interface between iterations.

Design decisions

Honesty as a design principle

Relocation tools tend to oversell certainty. Kiwi Compass does the opposite. The dataset is labelled as an indicative snapshot compiled from official sources, not live statistics. The home page carries a plain disclaimer: the results are a hint, not a guarantee - if you already have a job or family waiting, you will be happiest where they are.

The same honesty shapes the scoring. The citizenship question was a trap worth avoiding: the four-year direct pathway for NZ citizens is national law, identical in every city. Instead of implying Brisbane grants citizenship faster than Perth, the app scores cities on how easily you can build the stable base behind the pathway, and says so.

Craft

Accessible, installable, flag-coloured

WCAG AA inside a brand constraint

The palette comes from the two flags: #346EAA blue, #E13940 red, white and black. The red measures 4.3:1 on white - just under the AA threshold for body text - so it is reserved for large text and graphics, while blue carries every text-and-UI role at 5.3:1+. Selection states pair colour with check marks, focus is managed across the step flow, and every toggle announces its state.

Dark by default, light by choice

The app opens in a true-black dark theme with a persistent sun/moon toggle. The flag blue fails contrast on black, so dark mode lifts it to a compliant tint of the same hue - the kind of detail a palette swap gets wrong by default.

A web app that installs like a native one

Built as a dependency-free progressive web app: no framework, no build step, offline-capable via a service worker, installable from Safari to an iPhone home screen with its own compass icon - the needle points north-west, the heading from New Zealand to Australia.

Outcome

From blank sheet to shipped product

Kiwi Compass went from idea to a deployed, installable product: a nine-step questionnaire, a self-weighted twelve-factor matching engine covering twelve cities, a radar visualisation, and a data model with a documented source for every figure. The next step on the roadmap is refreshing the dataset automatically from the ABS Data API, keeping the client static and fast.
